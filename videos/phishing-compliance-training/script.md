# Phishing Compliance Training — Full Script

## Scene 0: Title / Brand Intro (~10s)
[VOICEOVER]
"Phishing: Spot It. Stop It. — A Cybersecurity Compliance Training."

## Scene 1: The Scale of the Threat (~50s)
[VOICEOVER]
"Every day, over 3.4 billion phishing emails are sent worldwide. And it only takes one click to compromise an entire organization.

Phishing isn't just the most common cyber attack — it's also the most effective. Ninety-one percent of all data breaches begin with a phishing email.

These aren't random attempts by amateurs. Sophisticated attackers craft convincing messages designed to bypass every security filter and trick even vigilant employees.

The good news? With the right knowledge, you can spot a phish before it ever reaches your inbox, or before you take the bait."

## Scene 2: What Is Phishing? (~50s)
[VOICEOVER]
"So, what exactly is phishing? At its core, phishing is a type of social engineering attack where cybercriminals impersonate a trusted entity to steal sensitive information — passwords, credit card numbers, or access credentials.

Think of it as digital bait. The attacker sends a lure — an email, text message, or phone call that looks legitimate — designed to hook you into clicking a malicious link, downloading an infected attachment, or revealing confidential data.

The term 'phishing' is a play on 'fishing' — because attackers are casting a line and waiting for someone to bite. And while some attacks are broad and untargeted, others are highly specific."

## Scene 3: Common Types of Phishing (~75s)
[VOICEOVER]
"Phishing comes in many forms. Let's break down the most common ones you'll encounter.

First, email phishing — the classic. You receive an urgent message that appears to be from your bank, IT department, or a trusted vendor. It asks you to verify your account, reset your password, or take immediate action. The link in the email leads to a fake login page designed to capture your credentials.

Next, spear phishing. Unlike broad email phishing, spear phishing targets you personally. The attacker researches your role, your team, or your projects to craft a message that feels legitimate and relevant. That makes it far more dangerous.

Then there's smishing — SMS or text message phishing — where attackers send fraudulent texts with links to malicious sites. And vishing, or voice phishing, where scammers call pretending to be from your IT help desk or a vendor, asking for sensitive information.

Whaling targets senior executives with carefully crafted messages about acquisitions, legal matters, or financial transactions. And business email compromise, or BEC, involves attackers posing as a CEO or vendor to request urgent wire transfers."

## Scene 4: Anatomy of a Phishing Email (~60s)
[VOICEOVER]
"Let's look at what a phishing email actually looks like. Watch for these telltale signs.

The sender address doesn't match the organization it claims to be from. It might be off by a character — go0gle.com instead of google.com.

The greeting is generic — 'Dear Customer' or 'Dear User' — instead of using your name.

The message creates urgency — 'Your account will be suspended within 24 hours' or 'Immediate action required.' Fear and time pressure are the phisher's best tools.

The email includes a suspicious link or attachment. Hover over any link without clicking — the actual URL rarely matches what the text claims.

And finally, there are often grammatical errors, awkward phrasing, or unusual formatting. Legitimate organizations proofread their communications."

## Scene 5: Top Watch-Outs — The Red Flags (~60s)
[VOICEOVER]
"Here are the top five red flags to watch for every time you check your inbox.

Number one: Unexpected requests for sensitive information. No legitimate organization will ask for your password, PIN, or social security number via email.

Number two: Emotional manipulation. Urgency, fear, curiosity, or excitement — phishers exploit your emotions to override your critical thinking.

Number three: Mismatched URLs. The text says one thing, but the actual link goes somewhere else. Always hover to verify.

Number four: Unsolicited attachments. If you weren't expecting a document or invoice, don't open it. That's how ransomware gets in.

Number five: Too good to be true. A prize you didn't enter, a refund you didn't request, a deal nobody else got — if it sounds too good to be true, it is."

## Scene 6: What To Do — Your Action Plan (~50s)
[VOICEOVER]
"So what should you do when you spot a phishing attempt?

First, do not click, reply, or open any attachments. Not even to 'unsubscribe.'

Second, report it immediately. Use your company's phishing reporting button or forward the email to your security team. Every report helps protect your colleagues.

Third, delete the message after reporting it. Don't forward it to coworkers or try to investigate it yourself.

And remember — if you do accidentally click, don't panic. Immediately notify your IT security team. Quick action can contain the damage and prevent a full breach.

At our organization, security is everyone's responsibility. When you stay vigilant, you protect not just yourself — but everyone around you."
